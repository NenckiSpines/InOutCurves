\section{Introduction}

In the brain, the primary function of neurons is to integrate, transform incoming information and reliably transfer processed information to other neurons. In the past decades, axons and dendrites have been considered to be simple transmitting devices that communicate signals to the cell soma in which thresholded computations take place. Electrophysiological recordings provided an important insight into the function of excitable cells and neural networks. Thus, in a simplified model, information carried by neural networks typically arrives at chemical synapses on dendrites or neuron’s soma where it induces an electrical response of the local membrane via neurotransmitter receptors and/or ion channels. Locally induced transmembrane electric currents change the membrane potential and these signals can be summed temporarily and spatially. Integration of all excitatory and inhibitory signals determines the activation of voltage-gated ion channels and transmembrane ion fluxes. When inward sodium current is not balanced by outward current leading to increasing membrane depolarization, action potential (AP) discharge may take place. The spatial distribution of APs is unique for a given type of neuron and codes for information sent to its target neurons via axon. 
Relationships between inputs and outputs in individual neurons or local networks can be experimentally studied with patch-clamp technique\cite{Nagaeva2021-jt} and local field potential recordings\cite{Tetteh2019-ts}. The systems comprising of amplifiers and digitizers used for field potentials recordings and single-cell patch-clamp recordings allow to record cell electrical activity while providing control over the inputs. Exogenous brief pulses of direct current stimuli may be used for direct activation of afferents (fibers) to promote release of neurotransmitter. In such a system, in most common data presentations, fiber volley amplitude which is proportional to number of activated afferent fibers (input) is plotted against evoked field excitatory synaptic potentials (fEPSPs, output). Alternatively, synaptic signals (fEPSPs, input) are plotted against population spike signal (PS, output) which  is proportional to the number of locally generated action potentials. While field potential recording allows to study networks of synaptically connected neurons, patch-clamp technique on the other hand provides resolution on the single cell level. Here action potentials of single neuron can be recorded in current-clamp mode in response to monotonically increasing current pulses applied though patch electrode\cite{Chaichim2021-wi}. In this method, the intracellular current stimuli (inputs) are typically plotted against the number of action potentials generated by neuron (output). 
In a common view, the number and spatial distribution of inputs such as active axons and synapses and the structure and complexity of dendrites, weights of synapses, are key for the shape of input-output function. However, in the recent years, it has been shown that passive membrane properties of neurons and many other parameters contribute to the complex input-output function of a neuron\cite{Yamazaki2022-lh}. For instance, gain modulation in dendritic spine heads, thresholded computations in small dendritic branchlets, and supralinearities at the main apical trunk and the cell body are additional examples of nonlinear computations performed by various parts of the neurons (reviewed by Kyriaki et al.\cite{Sidiropoulou2006-kh}). While mechanisms of synaptic plasticity have long tradition of studies, neurons exert a wide range of other less understood mechanisms allowing changes in intrinsic properties of the membrane, responsiveness to stimuli and ultimately firing pattern\cite{Zhang2003-ku, Yousuf2020-kp}. Thus, scaling of synaptic inputs upon plasticity does not linearly affect the neuronal output (i.e. number of action potentials) complicating prediction of input-output function of neurons, a phenomenon call EPSP-to-spike plasticity (E-S, reviewed by Wojtowicz et al.\cite{Wojtowicz2015-nb}). 

B) Why do we need to compare the curves

Input-output curves provide insight to how neurons transfer presynaptic activity into output. Studying these curves play important role in analysis of neuroactive compounds, drugs, and temporal or comparative studies in brain physiology or pathophysiology. For instance, relationship between synaptic input and the magnitude of neuronal spiking may be actively enhanced following some behavioural paradigms such has hippocampal learning\cite{Ohno2006-db} and impaired in a number of neurodevelopmental disorders including Fragile X Syndrome\cite{Booker2021-pd}. Simulation methods are also applied to examine how the inputs are transformed by neurons into function of brain regions such as the hippocampus\cite{Olypher2012-wq, De_Almeida2009-bf}. Proper methodological approach to analyse this phenomenon is critical for studies of brain physiology and pathophysiology as well as effects of drugs. However, input-output curves for various preparations show huge variability shapes and do not follow single-function rule. Thus, providing single universal equation and comparing data between experiments is not possible. For instance, some cortical pyramidal neurons exhibit linear input-output relationships (PMID: 26592167, PMID: 25568159) while neurons may exhibit sigmoidal relationship (GABAergic interneuron\cite{ROTH}, posteriomedial complex thalamic neuron\cite{Landisman}). In case of field-potential studies, similarly shape of the input-output functions is determined by region in the brain and experimental setup (PMID: 28052245, PMID: 26758828, PMID: 20630068, PMID: 11738933, PMID: 29311928). 
Importantly, there is also no standard and common practice how statistically compare input-output functions. Plotted data have been statistically analyzed with sigmoid fit (PMID: 12514207), linear fit (PMID: 25568159), one-way ANOVA (PMID: 31331291), two-way ANOVA~\cite{Klein2014-iz}, two-sided Wilcoxon signed rank test (PMID: 26592167, PMID: 32382046), two-way repeated measures ANOVA~\cite{Chaichim2021-wi}.
  
 
C) WHY IS THE STATISTICS /PARAMETRIC/ PROBLEMATIC?

Formally, the data represented bu in-out curves with different subjects in the experimental groups, are compatible with the structure of the mixed model ANOVA test with two different types of factor variables (between-subjects factors represented by the groups) and within-subjects factors (represented by the excitation voltage). The case of repeated measurements on the same subjects,  corresponds to the structure of two-way repeated measurements ANOVA with two within-subjects factors (excitation voltage, and e.g. pre/post treatment categorization.). The general case 

However, the test makes a number of restrictive assumptions regarding the data, such as normality, homogeneity of variances between the groups of the between-subjects factors, assumption of sphericity, i.e.,  the variance of the differences between within-subjects, homogeneity of covariances. Moveover, mixed model ANOVA is intended to be a method used to test both for statistical significance of both between-subjects factors and within-subjects factors. For the input-output curves, there is no question whether the excitation voltages is a statistically-significant factor. It is a'priori known, that the values of the output current drastically depend on the excitation voltage, and that in most cases, the associated variance is definitely larger than the between-subjects variance.
Other methods of comparison the input output curves include ANOVA, ACNOVA, comparison of regression curves \cite{Neumeyer}, or fitting sigmoid function to the inputo-output curves and performing statistics on the parameteres of the fit. 

Another important issue is the dependence of the test sensitivity on the number of subjects inthe experiment.

For all of the above approaches, certain assumptions for the characteristic of the data are made, either to fulfill the conditions of the parametric tests validity, or to match with the form of the fitted curve. On the other hand, nonparametric tests relax distribution and cross-correlation assumptions \cite{krzywinski}

\section{Non-parametric test}

In the following section, we present the computational details of the non-parametric test intended for the comparison of the input-ouput cirves. The test is based on data randomization, and calculation of the $L2$ norm.
Initially, for the reason of simplicity, we consider only two experimental groups, with different subjects in each group, possibly multiple samples per subject, and multiple measurements per sample. Later, the method is generealized to arbitrary number of groups. We also include the scenario of repeated measurements per subject, i.e., the case with two within subject factor, one of them being the excitation current. the second one representing, for example, pre/post treatment categorization. 

In the discussed design, the basic object with which we operate, is the input-output curve, denoted as $f_{g,s,m}(x)$, where $g$ denotes the experimental group, $s$ denotes the subject in the experimental group, $m$ denotes the number of the measrurement for the given subject and $x$ denotes the contiuous within-subject parameter (the excitation voltage). In case of two experimental groups, the experiment is represented by two sets of $f_{g=1,s,m}(x)$ and $f_{g=2,s,m}(x)$ curves, with possibly different number of subjects in each group, and different number of repetition for each subject. 
For the null hypothesis $H_0$, we assume that the input-output curves do not depend statistically on the experimental groups.  We represent the null hypothesis $H_0$ by the set of curves in the  randomized ensemble $\tilde{f}^{[k]}_{g,a,s}(x)$, where $k$ numbers the subsequent randomizations.

For the purpose of computing the p-value, we need to prepare a ranking, which includes the quantitative difference between the actual curves for both experimental groups, and the multiple values representing a set of differences between curves from the randomized ensemble. 
In order to make such a ranking possible, we need first to provide a quantitative measure of how much the curves deviate overall from each other.

To this end, we exploit $L2$ norm, defined as 
\begin{equation}
L2=\sqrt{\int^{x_{max}}_{x_{min}}(f_1-f_2)^2 dx},
\end{equation}
for two continuous curves $f_1$ and $f_2$, where $x_{min}$ and $x_{max}$ are the limits of the curves argument. In our case, we have curves represented by the discrete set of points, thus we compute the integral explicitely, using a linear interpolation for between-points values, see Materials and Methods.

\subsection{Randomized ensemble}
We perform the conditional randomization, creating new expermental gru

For the randomization tests, the finite and discrete nature of randomizations results in the discretization of p-value levels. This discretization can be smoothed out by increasing the number of randomizations. However, the maximum number of randomizations has been restricted to ensure acceptable computational time. In cases of extremely small p-values, any resulting p-value smaller than $0.001$ has been reported as "$p_{val}<0.001$" because precise p-values representing extreme results are sensitive to biases or limitations of precision in numerical representation.

\subsection{L2 measure}

The $\ell^2$-norm for two functions $f\left(x\right)$ and $g\left(x\right)$ on the interval
$\left[x_\text{min.},\;x_\text{max.}\right]$ is
\[\ell^2=\int_{x_\text{min.}}^{x_\text{max.}}\left(g\left(x\right)-f\left(x\right)\right)^2\text{d}x.\] \texttt{get\_l2}
operates on two piecewise linear functions $f\left(x\right)$ and $g\left(x\right)$ with equal sets of $n$ segment
intervals, $\left[x_0,\;x_1\right]$ through $\left[x_n,\;x_{n+1}\right]$. For an interval $\left[x_i,\;x_{i+1}\right]$,
where $i\in\left[0,\;n\right]$, the slope of the corresponding $f\left(x\right)$ segment is $\left(m_f\right)_i$, and
the $y$-intercept of the segment is $\left(b_f\right)_i$. Analogously, the slope of the corresponding $g\left(x\right)$
segment is $\left(m_g\right)_i$, and the $y$-intercept of the segment is $\left(b_g\right)_i$. We have
\[\ell^2=\sum_{i=0}^n\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}\left(\left(\left(m_g\right)_i-\left(m_f\right)_i\right)x+\left(b_g\right)_i-\left(b_f\right)_i\right)^2\text{d}x.\]
Let $m_i=\left(m_g\right)_i-\left(m_f\right)_i$, and $b_i=\left(b_g\right)_i-\left(b_f\right)_i$. Then,
\[\ell^2=\sum_{i=0}^n\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}\left(m_i x+b_i\right)^2\text{d}x.\] Integrating, we obtain
\[\ell^2 =\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left.\left(\frac{\left(m_i\right)^2x^3}{3}+m_ib_ix^2+\left(b_i\right)^2x\right)\right|_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}.\]
This is the formula used by \texttt{get\_l2}.

\subsection{simulated data}
Two diffrent classes of the simulated data have been constructed,
The set of two groups, statistically identical, is represented by the curves 
$\tilde{f}^{[k]}_{g,a,s}(x)=\sigma _{a}f^{BASE}(x)+\sigma _{n}(x)$, where $\sigma _{a}$ and $\sigma _{n}(x)$ are random variables representing variation of the slope of the input-output curves, and the uncorrrelated noise for each measurements. The curve $f^{BASE}(x)$ has been computed as the average curve in the control groups of the actual data. The random variable $\sigma _{a}$ /the slope/ is drawn from non-gaussian distribution, for each input-output curve. The random variable $\sigma _{n}(x)$ /the noise/ is drawn from the Gaussian distribution for each curve and excitation voltage. The parameters of both distributions, and the shape of the  $\sigma _{a}$ distribution, were calculated by performing the least squares fits to the actual data.
